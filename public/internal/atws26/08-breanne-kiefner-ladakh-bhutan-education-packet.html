What she told us

  • Groups of 8 to 12. Usually women, open to men, clientele 40 to 60. Active.
  • "We're anti bucket list." Not summiting something to say they summited it. Looking to connect.
  • The main focus is mindfulness — but explicitly not the Tibetan-mountain-retreat version. Her framing: how do people exist in a mindful way around the globe? Intentional presence while cooking, laughter, the ordinary.
  • She pushed back on the wellness resort, directly and usefully. Her clients need to be in the experience more than in their safety. A wellness property offers more comfort than she wants for them, because she thinks the real change happens outside comfort.
  • On the word wellness: hyper-commodified, and she does not believe in it. Everything she teaches should be free and accessible for clients to take home and share. A yoga class only makes sense with a genuinely local practitioner who is connected to the place — "somebody in their stretchy leggings" creates a barrier to the real thing, which she defines as mental health.
  • The hot stone baths interested her, on condition they feel appropriate to the place. They are a centuries-old farmers' practice here, not a tourist product, which is exactly the distinction she was drawing.
  • They can do multi-day hikes and be uncomfortable. Her people are curious, open, and all going through some kind of transition.
  • The honest blocker: "It's such a big area to know and I don't know when I'd be able to get there personally. I feel like I need to be there to sell it. I don't have my footing." She said plainly she is a little intimidated by the region.

What this changes about the deliverables

She did not ask for product. She described a knowledge gap and a timing problem — she cannot get there soon, and she will not sell what she does not understand.

So the educational packet is the real deliverable and the itineraries are illustrations inside it. Brian's framing in the room: our job is to lower the barrier to entry, because the region is not intimidating to us — we have been there 27 years. There is a lot she can understand before she ever lands, so that she is not waiting to arrive to start learning.

And: Ladakh leads, not Bhutan. Brian said it in the room after hearing her — Ladakh may be better suited for what you're looking for — because Ladakh requires some pain and suffering and it is purposeful.

Piece 1 — The educational packet

Section A — The single map that fixes the geography

Four countries, one range. Bhutan in the east, Ladakh in the far west, Nepal between. Nothing else. Most of the intimidation is not knowing what sits where relative to what.

Section B — How different the countries actually are

The thing nobody expects: one mountain range, and the cultures are not variations on each other. Ladakh is Tibetan Buddhist India. Bhutan is its own country with its own answer to why anyone should visit. Nepal is Hindu and Buddhist, dense and loud at the bottom and empty at the top. Not better or worse. Different.

Section C — Seasons, which decide everything

Season
Ladakh June, July, August only. Spring and fall out. Winter impassable.
Bhutan March–May and September–November. Summer is monsoon.
Nepal Spring and autumn

Two of the three are opposites, which is more useful than it sounds: a supplier with all three has something running most of the year.

Section D — Altitude, in plain language

Why it is the organising constraint and not a footnote. What acclimatisation actually involves, why two real days are built in rather than assumed, and why an operator who shortcuts it will cost a guest their trip. For her specifically: altitude enforces slowness. She spends her programmes teaching people to slow down; in Ladakh the terrain does it for her, and the acclimatisation days become programme time rather than dead time.

Section E — What guiding means here

Guides as the experience rather than a requirement. Day one he is your guide; by day three he is your friend; by the end it is family. For a group whose whole premise is connection, this is the part of the region that does the work.

Section F — What is actually hard

Permits, four different regimes, no infrastructure chain, weather, road, and the fact that almost nothing can be sourced locally. Written honestly, because her pushback on "wellness" suggests she will spot anything smoothed over.

Section G — Mindfulness without the industry

Bhutan is one of the most mindful countries you can visit and has been for centuries. It is not fabricated and it is not sold. Hot stone baths are what local farmers do. Monastery mornings are not a scheduled experience; they are Tuesday. This is the section that answers her objection directly — and it is worth saying that we are not trying to sell her a wellness property.

Piece 2 — Ladakh · the one that fits her brief

8 to 12 people. 12 days door to door, 9 on the ground. June to August.

  • Delhi, received by our own office
  • Nimmu, two acclimatisation days in a converted Ladakhi homestead — slow by design
  • Zanskar Valley for five days: day hikes, a multi-day option, palace ruins, mountain biking if wanted
  • Homestays that are functioning family homes. Meals morning and evening on the floor with the family, eating what they eat. Not a trekker circuit, not a staged cultural evening. This is the piece of the region that matches what she means by mindfulness better than anything we could design.
  • Leh at the end, then a buffer day

We manage altitude carefully for guests in their 60s and 70s on the active side — her 40 to 60 group has considerably more headroom than most of the people we run this for.

Piece 3 — Bhutan · offered, not pushed

Same 8 to 12, 10 to 12 days on the ground, March–May or September–November.

Paro, Thimphu, over Dochu La to Punakha, village-based days, Phobjikha.

On the lodge: it exists, it is small and intimate by design, and it is offered as an option rather than built into this itinerary. Brian's own words in the room: it may be that she does not want that space at all, and our itineraries do not have to include it. Village-based lodging carries this trip instead.

If any part of the property interests her it is likely the hot stone baths and the working farm — the two things on it that a Bhutanese farmer would recognise.

Before this goes out — Brian only

  1. Do not lead with the resort. She told us plainly it is not what she wants for her clients. The packet and the Ladakh piece are the deliverable; Bhutan is second and the lodge is a footnote inside it.
  2. The educational packet is a real piece of work — it is not a brochure with a new cover. Decide who writes it before promising a date.
  3. Getting her there. Brian said a big piece would be getting her on the ground to see it. That is the actual solution to her problem and it is not in this document. A fam invitation, dated, is worth more than all three pieces.
  4. Her own principles rule out most Himalayan product on the market — she rejects bucket-list itineraries, carbon offsets and multi-stop flights publicly. Anything we send must not read as a bucket-list trip with the language changed.
  5. Her personal history stays off every page. Her About page is candid about a great deal and it is hers to tell, not ours to pitch on. The existing build fails if any of it appears; keep that rule here.
  6. Root includes all gear rentals in the trip price and runs an eight-week pre-trip programme. Find out what we would have to provision in region before anyone claims we can match it — nothing here claims it.
  7. The property is not named.
