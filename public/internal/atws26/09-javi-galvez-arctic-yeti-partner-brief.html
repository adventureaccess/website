What he told us

  • He did not request the meeting. He said so in the first minute, and he was candid about why he took it anyway: he normally writes to cancel when there is no possible fit, and he did not. He was curious, and he wanted to learn what we do there.
  • Over the years more of his clients ask for something else, and he has always said no — this is what we do, it is all we can offer. He is starting to wonder whether this region could be something he considers in future.
  • Arctic Yeti is exclusively Nordic: Iceland, Greenland, Faroes, Norway, Finland, Sweden. Those destinations are not similar to each other — he was clear about that, which is exactly our experience of Ladakh versus Bhutan.
  • He moved to Greenland in 2001, lived there almost ten years. Brian moved to Tibet in 2001. Neither of them had to explain the rest: building an operation where you have to teach every single step.
  • He raised the ATTA guide standard himself, and the thing he actually wanted: he has met a number of companies through it and they exchange guides across seasons. Many of his guides are seasonal and very good, and a network of trustworthy people who fit each other's programmes is worth having.

The shape of this relationship

Third Pole and Arctic. Different cold, same problem: no infrastructure chain, so you become both ground operator and DMC because the region requires it. Same client personality at the far end of the funnel — people who ask where else, and who do you trust there.

Brian's framing in the room, and the right one: this is a connection to keep in the pocket and pull out when the opportunity comes. Not a campaign. It has been mentioned that Patagonia interests our client base as much as the Himalaya does, and his part of the world is not unrelated to that instinct.

A single page he can keep and use when a client asks him what else:
Who Adventure Access, founded 2009; Brian living and working in the region 27 years
Where Northern India (Ladakh, where the Himalaya meets the Karakoram), Nepal, Bhutan, and origins in eastern Tibet
Base Bhutan from late October — offices in India, Nepal, Bhutan and China
Structure Our offices, with local ownership inside them
Product No set departures. Everything private. The website is brochure mode — it shows what is possible, then we build
Length Typically 10 to 16 days on the ground, because Europeans and North Americans lose four days either end
Activity Hiking and trekking led, mountain biking where wanted, deep cultural immersion throughout. Day hikes to 19-day itineraries
Lodging Homestays to five star, client's choice
Guiding All core product guided; optional unguided add-ons front and back
Season, Bhutan March–May and September–November. Monsoon out.
Season, Ladakh June–August. Spring and fall out, winter impassable.
Not us Everest expeditions. That is a different industry.

The seasonal point matters for him specifically: Ladakh's window is June to August, which is his own peak Arctic season. Bhutan's is spring and autumn, which is not. If referrals ever flow, they flow into his shoulder.

2. The guide standard and guide exchange — his ask, not ours

This is the part he actually initiated and it should be treated as the most serious item here.

  • We have been part of ATTA for many years, and Brian has been on the education side — including the team ATTA ran with the Japan National Tourism Bureau.
  • Our operations are hungry for the guide standard. Adventure Access runs two sides: one develops entrepreneurs, operators and guides across the region; the other is client-facing. The development side is what makes the client-facing side trustworthy, because we built the system ourselves rather than buying it in.
  • What to propose: a first conversation on how his exchange network actually works in practice — what a placement looks like, what he expects of a visiting guide, what the seasons are on each side. Our seasonal shape and his are close to complementary.

This is the item most likely to produce something real within a year, and it costs nothing but a call.
