What he told us

  • They have done Ladakh a million times. They released a new Ladakh trip that is not selling this year and he said, without prompting, "it definitely needs improvement."
  • Nepal for fifty years, mostly trekking, and those were their biggest sellers. Now a harder sell: their clientele has aged into the 60s, and the younger demographic they used to get is going direct — a thousand dollars for two weeks on a screen, and they book it.
  • Tibet re-released in the last few years, sold only as Tibet-to-Nepal or Tibet-to-Bhutan combinations. Using a Tibetan partner they had not worked with in a decade. First trip is imminent and he is watching it closely.
  • Bhutan they have done forever and they are happy with their outfitter — Kesang, formerly on the tourism board, and Gangri Tours. He named them without hesitation.
  • He asked twice for our read on where the industry is going, and asked directly whether we have anything in Nepal for people who want real hiking without camping and without the standard lodges.
  • He has been to Ladakh twice. He called it a fascinating cultural area and said outright: "we want to be selling it, but we're not doing a great job. I think we just need to get our product right."
  • Family business. He grew up going to the Himalaya.

The frame

He does not need a destination sold to him. The right question is not would they sell what we have but what operational gaps could we fill. There is exactly one obvious gap and he named it himself: their Ladakh product.

Nothing in this document tries to move him off Kesang or Gangri in Bhutan. That was said in the room and it should stay true in writing.

1. Why their Ladakh trip underperforms — a hypothesis, offered as one

Most Ladakh product is built around the classic route, and the classic route is where everyone goes. Indian domestic tourism runs north and east — the famous lake, the famous photograph, the crowds. A trip built there is competing on price against operators with a lower cost base, which is precisely the pressure he described.

Our operation runs west, into Zanskar, and that is not a second-choice workaround. We are aware the better options are there; the crowds are not, because Zanskar is harder to reach, needs more time, and requires guides who actually understand acclimatisation. You cannot run mass volume through it. That is the barrier, and for a company like theirs the barrier is the asset.

He asked in the room whether Zanskar is still accessible without committing to a full trek. It is, and it has changed a lot. The Chadar trek — the frozen-river walk most people associated with Zanskar — is effectively gone, which is why the valley dropped off many operators' maps. What is there now is different and, for an aging active clientele, better.

2. What we run in Zanskar

  • Day hikes and multi-day hikes, so the same departure serves guests at two fitness levels
  • Non-technical summit options — physically demanding, no climbing skill; the difficulty is altitude
  • Mountain biking, which matters more than it used to (see below)
  • Palace and fort ruins, so day hikes have a destination rather than a viewpoint
  • Curated homestays with families we work with exclusively — functioning family homes, meals on the floor with the household, clean and updated bathrooms, not luxury and not pretending to be
  • Culture that is genuinely intact rather than staged for a circuit

3. Acclimatisation as product design, not a disclaimer

Tours are curated around altitude and around experience, deliberately.

  • Two real acclimatisation days, based out at Nimmu rather than in Leh — a converted Ladakhi noble homestead run by a couple we have a long relationship with
  • We do not shortcut it. The alternative is a guest on supplemental oxygen on day three and a ruined itinerary
  • The aim is that nobody loses days to feeling ill, which for a 60-something clientele is the difference between a trip that reviews well and one that does not

For a company whose guests want activity but not a 10-day sufferfest, this is the specific operational thing that is hard to buy and easy to get wrong.

4. On the aging demographic — the answer to what he asked

Our read, since he asked for it twice:

  • The demographic has aged up, and activity levels have softened with it. Those two move together.
  • It has not reduced the appetite for activity. They want more comfort in the lodging and are perfectly willing to go out and suffer for a few days — just not ten days consecutively.
  • Ladakh and Bhutan both serve that better than Nepal does, and we sell Bhutan far better than Nepal for exactly this reason.
  • On bikes: people in their 50s and 60s taking up cycling as a serious pursuit is now a real segment. What looks reasonable for a 60-year-old on a bike, they want to do. We run mountain biking in Zanskar and it is worth him knowing.

On Nepal, candidly. The structural issue is that the community model there equalises the product — local families are discouraged from standing out, so the menus are the same, the lodging is the same, and differentiation is removed at source. It is culturally driven and not something an operator can fix; it can only be routed around. That is why our Nepal promotion is selective rather than broad.

5. The resort — what he asked for and what we promised

He asked the name. There is not one yet — that is the honest answer and it was given in the room.

What is true today:
Where Central Bhutan, in the valley between Phobjikha and Wangdue, about an hour outside Punakha. Off the main circuit.
Why it is interesting Quieter than Phobjikha, and we are the only rooms there
Setting 15-acre working farm, adjacent to a protected Ramsar wetland
Size 16 standard rooms, three villas
What makes it different Dedicated programme space — yoga retreats, running camps, group programming. Most Bhutanese properties have rooms and a restaurant and nowhere to run anything.
Wellness Full spa, active recovery, traditional hot stone baths
Opening Soft opening in the spring, fully operational by summer, fall bookings underway
Who A three-partner project: Brian, a Bhutanese partner, and a long-standing ATTA figure based in Kathmandu — around 30 years of combined experience

The position, stated clearly: not a replacement for his operator or his itinerary. The question is whether a few nights of ours can become part of an itinerary Kesang and Gangri continue to run. He is welcome to put it to them directly, and we are happy to talk to them ourselves.
