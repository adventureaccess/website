Who this is for

Your Churchill guests, asking where else

Roughly fifteen hundred people a year go north with you to stand in front of polar bears, and a good number of them come back asking where else they can go with you. They are not a new audience to be found; they are a list you already hold. What they have in common is that wildlife is the reason they book and that most of them would rather see one thing properly than six things quickly. Both itineraries below are built for the slower end of that range, on the assumption that nothing on the route should require a guest to prove anything.

The problem worth solving first

Your season and Bhutan's best season are the same three weeks

Peak black-necked crane viewing runs late October into November. That is precisely your Churchill window — eleven to thirteen one-day charters out of four Canadian cities, on a very small team. You cannot be running a Bhutan departure inside it, and we would not ask you to.

So rather than send you a single itinerary that quietly ignores the clash, here are two, each honest about what it can and cannot promise.

Ninety minutes of road, then an afternoon with the takin — Bhutan's national animal, found nowhere else on earth, and a genuinely strange-looking one. For a group whose reference point is a polar bear, it lands. Easy walking on made paths throughout.

The pass at 10,200 ft is the highest point on the route and the group crosses it sitting down. On a clear late-November morning the eastern Himalaya is laid out behind the chortens. Then a long descent into Punakha at 4,300 ft, which is noticeably warm — a relief rather than an adjustment. Punakha Dzong late in the afternoon, when the light is on it.

  • Day 5: Birding along the Punatsangchhu — the river system associated with the white-bellied heron, one of the rarest large birds on earth
  • Flat ground, short distances, the vehicle never far
What we will not claim

No operator can promise a white-bellied heron, and we will not put one in a brochure. What this day offers is the right river, at the right hour, at a pace that lets people actually look. Anything beyond that is luck, and your guests will respect being told so.

✦ The centre of the trip

About an hour from Punakha. Arrive early and settle in — a hot stone bath in the evening, which here is a farmers' tradition rather than a spa invention, and that distinction matters to this audience.

The lodge sits on a fifteen-acre working farm adjacent to a Ramsar-designated protected wetland. In crane season the birds are neighbours: visible from a room balcony, or a flat stroll off the property. There is nothing to arrange, no dawn convoy, no hide, no four a.m. start. For a seventy-eight-year-old who has spent a week in a tundra buggy, being able to see the thing from a chair is not a compromise — it is the product.

  • Day 8 — Phobjikha: day trip to the wider bowl and Gangtey. The valley nature trail is flat and runs about ninety minutes unhurried, with a turn-back point at any stage
  • Day 9 — unstructured: farm-to-table kitchen, a village visit, or nothing at all. For this group one genuinely empty day inside the itinerary does more for satisfaction than another sight

What changes

Cranes stop being the promise

They are usually gone by mid-March, sometimes lingering. If the group hits stragglers it is a gift, not a line in the brochure. We would rather your guests arrive hoping than arrive owed.

Rhododendron, and the passes at their clearest

Dochu La in March is the clear-skies photograph of the trip. For a group that travels with cameras, this is the single strongest argument for spring over fall.

The takin day expands

From an afternoon to a proper half-day, which the spring schedule has room for.

The lodge becomes the recovery middle, not the peak

Three nights rather than four. Without the cranes it stops being the reason for the trip and becomes the place the trip rests in the middle — which is arguably what this audience needs from it anyway.

An optional southern extension, for the fitter half only

Bhutan's south is jungle: Bengal tigers, rhinos, elephants, and the scenes people picture from The Jungle Book. Country-wide it is only about a hundred miles from Himalaya to jungle as the crow flies, considerably more by road.

Flagged, not built

We have deliberately not written this extension into the itinerary. Before it goes anywhere near a brochure we need to answer three things honestly: whether we can operate it ourselves, to what standard of road and lodging, and whether it is survivable for a seventy-eight-year-old. If the answer to the third is no, we will cut it rather than soften it.
