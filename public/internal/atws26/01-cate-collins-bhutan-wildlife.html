What she told us

  • Two brands. Classic Canadian Tours is Canada. Gold Star runs Tanzania and Rwanda safaris.
  • ~1,500 guests a year to Churchill for polar bears. Those guests come back asking where else can we go with you. That repeat pool is the buyer, not a new audience.
  • Demographic is 65 to 85 and generally slow moving. Some active individuals, but build for the slower end.
  • Wildlife is the buying criterion. Some Canadian product is cultural, but wildlife is the spine.
  • Her blackout: late October to mid-November. That is prime polar bear season — 11 to 13 one-day charters to Churchill from four Canadian cities, on a very small team. She cannot be running a Bhutan departure inside it.

The scheduling problem, stated plainly

Peak black-necked crane viewing is late October into November — the same three weeks as her Churchill season. That collision is the single most important fact in this document.

Two ways around it, and both are below:

Option Window Crane situation
A — Late fall Second half of November, after Churchill closes Cranes are in and settled. Best wildlife case.
B — Spring March Cranes departing — possible, not promisable. Other wildlife leads.

Early September was also raised in the room as a fall alternative ahead of her season. It sits at the tail of the monsoon, so it is the weakest of the three for viewing and is not built out here.

Itinerary A — Late November · Cranes and valley floors

11 days on the ground, 13 door to door. Max altitude on the route: 10,200 ft, and only in a vehicle.

Day 1 — Arrive Paro

Fly in via Kathmandu, Bangkok, Delhi or Singapore. The Kathmandu–Paro leg is 45 minutes along the Himalayan wall and is worth booking deliberately for this group — it is a wildlife-audience moment in its own right. Met at the aircraft steps, short transfer, nothing scheduled after.

Day 2 — Paro, easy

Kyichu Lhakhang and the valley floor. Optional 40-minute flat riverside walk. Deliberately empty day; 7,300 ft is modest but it is still day one after a long flight.

Day 3 — Paro to Thimphu

90 minutes of road. Afternoon at the takin preserve — Bhutan's national animal, found nowhere else, and a genuinely strange-looking one. Easy walking on made paths.

Day 4 — Thimphu to Punakha over Dochu La

The pass at 10,200 ft, in the vehicle, with the eastern Himalaya laid out behind the chortens on a clear late-November morning. Descend to Punakha at 4,300 ft — noticeably warm. Punakha Dzong late afternoon.

Day 5 — Punakha river day

Birding along the Punatsangchhu. This is the river system associated with white-bellied heron, one of the rarest large birds on earth. See note below on what may and may not be claimed here. Flat ground, short distances, vehicle never far.

Day 6 — Punakha to the lodge

About an hour. Arrive early, settle in. Hot stone bath in the evening — a farmers' tradition here rather than a spa invention, which matters to this audience.

Day 7 — Cranes, from the property

The lodge sits on a 15-acre working farm adjacent to a Ramsar-designated protected wetland. In the crane season the birds are neighbours: visible from a room balcony, or a flat stroll off the property. Nothing to arrange, no dawn convoy, no hide.

Day 8 — Phobjikha

Day trip to the wider Phobjikha bowl and Gangtey. The valley nature trail is flat and runs about 90 minutes at an unhurried pace, with a turn-back point at any stage.

Day 9 — Lodge, unstructured

Farm-to-table kitchen, village visit, or nothing at all. For a 65–85 group, one genuinely empty day inside the itinerary does more for satisfaction than another sight.

Day 10 — Return west to Paro

Long road day broken by stops. Evening in Paro.

Day 11 — Paro, buffer

Held deliberately. Domestic weather delays are the normal failure mode here and a buffer day is cheaper than a missed international connection.

Day 12–13 — Depart

Itinerary B — March · Spring, without relying on cranes

Same spine, different wildlife argument. 10 days on the ground.

The changes from Itinerary A:

  • Cranes are not the promise. They are usually gone by mid-March, sometimes lingering. If the group hits stragglers it is a gift, not a line in the brochure.
  • Rhododendron and spring passes. Dochu La in March is the clear-skies photograph of the trip.
  • The takin day expands into a proper half-day.
  • Optional southern extension (3 days), for the fitter half only. Bhutan's south is jungle: Bengal tigers, rhinos, elephants, and the scenes people picture from The Jungle Book. Country-wide it is only about 100 miles from Himalaya to jungle as the crow flies, considerably more by road. This extension is flagged, not built — see the checklist.
  • The lodge stay stays at three nights and becomes the recovery middle rather than the wildlife peak.

What is deliberately not in either itinerary

Snow leopard. It is the hard end of the spread — Ladakh in February, at altitude, in the cold, on foot. It exists, we run in that country, and it is the wrong recommendation for most of her list. Raise it in conversation as the top of the ladder; do not build a 65–85 departure around it.

Her own interest in the mountain biking. She said the MTB product looks incredible personally. That is a conversation for her, not a page for her guests.

  2. White-bellied heron. Do not claim sightings. Confirm what may honestly be said about the Punatsangchhu before Day 5 goes to her in writing.
  3. The southern jungle extension — is it operable by us, at what standard of road and lodging, and is it survivable for a 78-year-old? If not, cut it rather than soften it.
